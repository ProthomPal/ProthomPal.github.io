Case study 01 · Google Play · Casual time-management

Hitting a retention target, build by build

How successive builds of My Cooking Story resolved every major level-design wall found along the way and carried Day-1 retention past its target — one level, one funnel and one loading theory at a time.

SECTION 01

The starting point

My Cooking Story is a tycoon-style cooking and time-management game: players build a restaurant across sequential themes, serving customers within a level's timer and target. The team's north-star metric was Day-1 retention against an internal benchmark — the share of new players who come back the day after installing.

My job was to move that number without a rewrite. That meant treating the level list as a tunable system rather than a fixed sequence of content, and finding out where the game was losing people before deciding what to change.

Across successive builds I tracked level-by-level progression, funnel depth, session loading behaviour and retention side by side, using each build's data to find the next lever before moving on.

Every build answered one question and raised the next. That loop is the whole story.

SECTION 02

The working loop

Rather than shipping a batch of changes and hoping, each build was scoped to answer one question. Ship, read the funnel, find the deepest wall, retune it, re-measure. Slower per build, but it meant every result could be attributed to something specific instead of being lost in a pile of simultaneous edits.

One question per build

Re-checking the same levels build over build is what let each wall be caught rather than assumed fixed.

SECTION 03

Resolving the level-design walls

Three separate difficulty spikes surfaced across the builds, each at a different point in the early-to-mid game. Tracking the same levels every build shows each one being found and resolved in turn — and, in one case, being reintroduced by a change made elsewhere.

What a wall looks like in the completion line

Schematic. Illustrates the shape of the problem and the fix, not measured values.

The first wall — early onboarding

The baseline build had a hard stall in the first few levels, before players had learned the loop well enough to know what they were doing wrong. This is the most expensive place in the game to lose someone: they haven't seen a restaurant come together yet, so there's nothing pulling them back. Retuning the timer, customer target and order complexity here cleared it, and it has held ever since.

The second wall — a regression, not a new problem

A later build introduced a stall at a level that had been fine before. Nothing had been changed at that level directly; a tuning change further upstream had shifted what players arrived with. That's the argument for re-measuring the whole early game every build rather than only checking what you touched.

The third wall — deeper in the ramp

The last spike sat further into the mid-game, on a pair of consecutive levels. Because it came after the two earlier walls were resolved, more players were reaching it — which is exactly why fixing an early wall can make a later one look worse before it gets better. Retuning that pair was the change that finally moved the retention number.

Difficulty spikes need to be tracked, not just fixed once.

SECTION 04

Funnel depth

Fixing each wall shows up directly in how far players get before quitting. Comparing the same funnel across builds is the clearest way to see whether a level change actually bought progression or just moved the drop somewhere else.

How the funnel changed shape

Earlier build Current build

Schematic. Shows the direction of change, not measured values.

Averaged across the early-to-mid game, the current build also holds the best mean completion and the lowest mean drop-off of any build measured — the walls aren't just resolved individually, the ramp as a whole is flatter.

SECTION 05

Design framework: reward pacing

Level design and reward cadence are tuned together, following a three-act structure across the game's level range. Onboarding rewards every clear to build habit; the mid-game spaces rewards out to create breathing room between pushes; the late game shifts to bigger, rarer payouts that reward mastery rather than frequency.

Late game

Reward cadence by act — the reward-frequency pattern the level list follows.

This cadence is deliberately asymmetric: dense, predictable rewards while players are still learning the core loop, widening as mastery increases, and a switch to weight-over-frequency in the endgame where players are optimising rather than discovering.

One rule sits underneath all of it — upgrade unlocks and new-content introductions are kept off the same level as each other wherever possible, so no single level asks a player to absorb two new things at once. Most of the early walls traced back to levels that broke this rule.

SECTION 06

Design framework: currency sources & sinks

The soft-currency loop follows a standard tycoon-economy structure: a small number of high-volume sources feed a small number of high-value sinks, keeping the loop legible without over-simplifying player choice.

The soft-currency loop, by weight

Sources
  1. Level-clear coins backbone
  2. Milestone chests interval
  3. Daily login bonus return hook
  4. Ad-watch bonus optional
Sinks
  1. Station upgrades primary
  2. Container upgrades secondary
  3. Speed boosters optional
  4. Item unlocks gated

Ranked by share of currency earned and spent across a typical playthrough. Order shown; proportions not published.

On the source side, level-clear rewards are the backbone of the loop — a predictable, always-available baseline. Milestone chests sit on top at set intervals, a daily login bonus anchors return visits, and an optional ad-watch bonus is there for players who want to accelerate without paying.

On the sink side, station upgrades are deliberately the primary spend, because they visibly and immediately improve throughput. The player sees the restaurant get faster, which is what keeps the upgrade loop satisfying. Container upgrades follow, then speed boosters for players in a hurry, and item unlocks that gate new recipes behind progression.

SECTION 07

Investigation: ruling out load time

Early on, a drop in the onboarding funnel looked like it might be a load-time problem rather than a design one — a reasonable theory, and one that would have sent the work in a completely different direction. The build carried loading-time telemetry for dropped sessions, so it could be tested directly instead of argued about.

Testing the theory

Hypothesis

Players are dropping at level load because the game is slow to start a level.

Test

Load time recorded for every dropped session, then checked against each level's drop-off rate.

Verdict — ruled out

The overwhelming majority of dropped sessions loaded fast, with no correlation between a level's load time and its drop-off.

The few slow sessions were isolated, not concentrated on any particular level or device pattern. That closed the line of investigation with confidence and sent the work back to level tuning, where the actual problem was.

Data can rule out a theory as usefully as it confirms one.

SECTION 08

Where it landed

Every fix above has to answer one question: does it move the metric the team is actually held to? It did.

Day-1 retention cleared its internal target with room to spare, on an install cohort large enough that this reflects a genuine shift rather than a favourable sampling window. Most of the game's live markets clear the target independently, and the one that doesn't sits just short of it — which matters more than the headline, because it's the evidence that the underlying game moved, not a single lucky market.

The retention gain also arrived later than the level-design gain. The number held flat through the build that resolved the second wall, and only jumped once the third was fixed and the funnel got meaningfully deeper. Not every improvement shows up immediately in the top-line metric — which is an argument for tracking progression directly rather than judging every change by retention alone.

Outcome

My Cooking Story moved from an unstable early build to a validated, retention-positive live game. Every major level-design wall found in the process was resolved, and Day-1 retention now sits comfortably above its target.

SECTION 09

What I took from it

Track spikes, don't assume they stay fixed.

Three separate walls surfaced across the builds, one of them reintroduced by a change made somewhere else entirely. Re-checking the same levels every build is what caught it.

Ruling a theory out is real progress.

The load-time investigation produced no fix and no metric movement, and it was still one of the most useful things I did — it stopped the team spending a build on the wrong problem.

Fixing an early wall can make a later one look worse.

More players surviving the early game means more players arriving at the next spike. Progression data has to be read as a whole funnel, not level by level in isolation.

Compounding small fixes beat one big swing.

No single change carried the target. Scoping each build to one question is slower, but it's the only way to know which change did what.
