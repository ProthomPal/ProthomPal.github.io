Case study 01 · Google Play

Finding out where players quit, and fixing it

My Cooking Story was losing new players early. Over four builds I worked out where they were dropping off and retuned the levels until Day-1 retention got past its target.

Play on Google Play ↗ Rendered Ideas · Simulation

From the Google Play listing. Click to enlarge.

Role
Game Designer
Focus
Progression & economy
Platform
Google Play
Genre
Cooking time management
Status
Beta testing
01

The problem

You run a restaurant, cook orders before the timer runs out, and upgrade the kitchen as you go. There's a slot system that lets you prep food early and hold it, so the skill is planning ahead rather than tapping fast.

The team tracked one number above everything else: how many new players came back the next day. We were under target.

No rewrite and no new features were on the table. What I had was the level list and the numbers behind it.

02

How I worked

I gave each build one question to answer. Ship a change, read the funnel level by level, find the worst drop, retune it, then check the whole early game again on the next build.

One question per build

Slower per build, but when retention moved I knew what moved it.

03

Three walls

Three difficulty spikes turned up over the four builds, each in a different part of the early game.

What a wall looks like in the completion line

Schematic, not measured values.

The first one, in onboarding

The baseline build stalled players in the first few levels, before they knew the game well enough to work out what they were doing wrong. It's the worst place to lose someone, because they haven't seen the restaurant come together yet. I retuned the timer, the customer target and the order complexity and it cleared.

The second one came back on its own

A later build broke a level that had been fine. I hadn't touched it. A tuning change earlier in the list had changed what players arrived with. That's why I started re-checking the whole early game every build instead of only the levels I'd edited.

The third one was further in

The last spike sat on two levels back to back, in the mid game. It looked worse than before, because fixing the earlier walls meant more players were now getting that far. Retuning those two was the change that finally moved retention.

04

Reward pacing and the economy

Levels and rewards get tuned together. Early levels pay out every clear to build the habit. The mid game spreads rewards out so there's a gap to push through. The late game pays less often but bigger, for players who are optimising rather than still learning.

Early game
Reward every level
Mid game
Milestone rewards at set intervals
Late game
Bigger, rarer payouts

One rule sits under all of it: an upgrade unlock and a new content introduction don't land on the same level if I can help it, so no level asks a player to take in two new things at once. Most of the early walls turned out to be levels that broke that rule.

The currency loop is a normal tycoon setup. A few sources feed a few sinks, and I kept both lists short so players can hold the whole thing in their head.

Sources and sinks, in order of weight

Sources
  1. Level clear coins backbone
  2. Milestone chests interval
  3. Daily login bonus return hook
  4. Ad watch bonus optional
Sinks
  1. Station upgrades primary
  2. Container upgrades secondary
  3. Speed boosters optional
  4. Item unlocks gated

Order shown. Actual proportions aren't published.

Station upgrades are the main sink on purpose. You buy one and the kitchen visibly gets faster, so spending feels good rather than like paying a tax.

05

The load time theory

Early on we thought the drop in onboarding might be a loading problem rather than a design one. It was a reasonable guess and it would have sent the work somewhere completely different. The build recorded load times for sessions that dropped, so I could check instead of argue.

Testing it

Theory

Players quit at level load because the game is slow to start a level.

Test

Load time for every dropped session, checked against each level's drop-off.

Result

Almost all of them loaded fast, and load time didn't line up with drop-off anywhere.

The few slow sessions were scattered, not clustered on a level or a device. It closed the question and sent me back to the levels, which is where the problem actually was. Ruling something out isn't a wasted build.

06

Where it ended up

Day-1 retention got past its target on a cohort big enough to trust, and most of the live markets clear it on their own rather than one market carrying the average. Players also get further into the level list than in any build we'd measured before.

What I'd do differently

Retention didn't move when I expected. It sat flat through the build that fixed the second wall and only jumped after the third one. I spent a while assuming that build had failed. Progression data would have told me it was working; I just wasn't looking at it as closely as the headline number.

I'd also check the whole early game after every tuning change from the start, not after a level broke and taught me to.

Case study 02 · Fire TV & Fire Tablet

BR

A list of fun mechanics isn't a game

Brainrot is a creature collection game for cheap living room hardware. I was the only designer on a team of six: I specced the systems and set the numbers, and our developer built them. Most of what I know now came from getting the first version wrong.

01

The first design was a list of verbs

You explore, find creatures, catch or steal them, fight in short timing based encounters, level them up, and decide what to keep. The pitch was simple enough: everything you find could be yours, and everything you own could be worth more than it is.

My first design was a list of things that sounded fun. Explore, discover, catch, fight, sell, buy eggs, build a collection. Each one was good on its own, which is exactly why I didn't notice the problem. They were fun to me imagining the game, not to a player forty minutes into their third session.

Discovery is exciting once. The fiftieth creature only matters if the game has given you a way to judge it.

Before: a sequence of events

Explore→ Find→ Catch→ Fight→ Upgrade

Nothing here asks the player anything.

After: a sequence of decisions

Explore→ Find→ Catch or steal→ Fight→ Level up who→ Sell, keep or merge→ Spend or save→ Buy egg

Amber nodes are the questions the player has to answer

Fixing it meant giving every creature things you could weigh up: stats, rarity, a level, a tier, whether it's worth merging. A find became a small decision instead of a small animation.

02

Tiers and merging

Collection games produce duplicates constantly, and a duplicate is normally a small disappointment. Merging turns that around. Two of the same creature combine into the next tier up, so a duplicate is half an upgrade instead of clutter.

The tier ladder

Base
Silver
Gold
Diamond
Shiny
God

You can see the top of the ladder in your first hour and you're nowhere near it, which is the point.

Merging also needs a minimum level, which took me a while to work out. Without it you merge the moment you get a duplicate and it's just inventory tidying. With it you have to invest in a creature before you can consolidate it, which slows tier inflation and pushes duplicates back through the levelling loop.

Tiers also solved a production problem. Six tiers across a shared set of creatures multiplies how much content the game appears to have without multiplying the art.

03

Making damage and theft matter

My first version healed everyone after every fight. Convenient, and it made combat pointless. Losing cost you a few seconds and nothing else.

So damage sticks around, HP comes back over real time, and healing centres speed that up if you don't want to wait. The loss is real but you pay for it in patience, not in losing the creature you like.

Two bad extremes, and where I landed

Stealing came from a similar problem. A collection that only grows has no stakes, so I made creatures stealable. The moment I did that, shields became necessary. A risk system with no way to defend yourself isn't tension, it's just losing things at random.

The awkward part is that the game has no live multiplayer. I had to get the feeling of a world where other people can take your stuff without the servers or the player numbers that would normally come with it.

04

What the data changed

The biggest shift in how I work came from measuring things. I wrote the spec for what the game needed to track, our developer wired it up, and then I had to sit with what players actually did rather than what I thought they'd do.

Before

"I think players will like this." Features added because they seemed appealing.

After

"Players are doing this. What design decision would explain it?"

Three things came out of that:

The tutorial was teaching too much at once. Everything it taught was correct, but there was too much of it too early. The fix was fewer ideas, not better wording.

A connectivity popup was costing us players at a specific point. It wasn't gameplay and I'd mentally filed it as not my problem. Players don't make that distinction. Anything that interrupts them is part of the experience.

Our milestone rewards were being ignored. Not disliked, ignored. Players didn't notice they had rewards waiting. A reward nobody claims isn't a reward, it's work we did for nothing.

05

When performance became my problem

The game targets cheap hardware, and the frame rate fell apart when a lot of creatures were on screen. Which is to say, exactly when the game was doing the thing it exists to do.

Our developer profiled it and worked through the rendering costs. My side of it was deciding how many creatures could be visible at once, when they show up, and how a big collection gets displayed, all inside whatever budget that left.

Creature count is a design decision, not just a tech one

Schematic, not measured data.

Fifty creatures that look great and run at ten frames a second isn't a good design. On hardware this cheap I can't hand a spec over and treat what it costs as someone else's problem.

06

What I got wrong

The short version of the whole project, in the order I learned it.

What I assumedWhat happenedWhat I changed
Simple combat would be enoughFights weren't engagingDirectional QTE with real timing
More creatures means more motivationCollecting alone stopped matteringStats, rarity and tiers
Duplicates are uselessPlayers pile them up anywayMerging above a level threshold
HP should just resetLosing cost nothingDamage sticks, heals over time
Stealing creates excitementIt created resentmentShields, so there's counterplay
Players will notice objectivesThey forgot they existedBetter visibility and ordering
More levels means more engagementDrop-offs clustered at specific pointsLevel by level analysis
More creatures on screen looks betterFrame rate collapsedCapped counts, display built to fit

The pattern I keep coming back to: every system I add creates a problem for the next one. Stealing needs protection. Protection creates scarcity. Scarcity needs an economy. The skill isn't getting the first version right, it's spotting the next problem before a player finds it for me.

Project 03 · Design direction

Goon Fight

Every level is one closed room with a fixed camera. No scrolling, no reveals, nowhere to hide a difficulty curve. Everything has to come from the layout, the enemies in each wave, and where they're placed.

Play on Google Play ↗ Rendered Ideas · Action

From the Google Play listing. Click to enlarge.

Role
Level Designer
Structure
Single room, fixed camera
Owned
Layouts, enemy types, waves, DDA tuning
Environments
Dungeon, jungle, village, moving bus
Tools
Unity
01

Level design

The camera doesn't move. The whole fight happens in what you can see when the level loads, which takes away most of the usual level design tools. No corridors to pace someone through, no corner to put a surprise behind. What's left is the shape of the room and where things stand in it.

Levels get built from three pieces:

The construction kit

Ten grids of screen width to work with, six grids of usable height above the floor.

The rule I held to across most of the game is that a player can reach any part of the floor or any standing platform in two jumps. It keeps the room feeling like one space instead of a set of separate ledges, and it means enemy placement is doing the work rather than traversal.

A few levels break it on purpose. Somewhere near the top of the map is a platform you can't get to in two jumps, and the first time you try you fall short. That's the point. Once a player has learned a rule, taking it away for one level is one of the only tools a fixed camera leaves you for creating surprise.

Two jumps, except when it isn't

The other hard constraint is spawn separation. Player spawn and enemy spawns never sit close together, because in a room this size a bad spawn means the fight starts with a hit the player couldn't have avoided.

02

Encounter design

I designed the enemy roster to cover different ranges and different kinds of pressure, so a wave can be built out of contrasts rather than more of the same thing.

Airborne chargers

Come at you across the room and ignore the floor layout entirely.

Brawlers

Close range, straightforward, there to occupy your hands.

Snipers

Long range and stationary. Punish you for standing still.

Grenade throwers

Short range area denial. Push you off wherever you're comfortable.

The wave problem

In a scrolling game you always roughly know where the player is, because they're moving forward. In a single room they could be anywhere when a wave ends. I'm placing the next wave without knowing where they're standing, which means placement is a guess unless you can shape it.

So I built the waves to pull players toward a position rather than trying to predict one. Two things do the pulling:

Visual weight. Bigger, louder, more obviously dangerous enemies draw attention first. Players go where the threat looks biggest, so putting weight on one side of the room reliably moves them to that side.

Enemies that are genuinely annoying. Snipers and grenade throwers don't look like the biggest threat, but a few seconds into the wave the player works out that they have to deal with them or nothing else will go well. That realisation happens mid fight, and it moves them.

Shaping the wave instead of guessing

Place visual weight→ Add an annoying enemy→ Player ends up here→ Build the next wave around it

Right about 60% of the time, which is enough to design against

Sixty percent isn't certainty, but it's a long way from nothing. It means the next wave can be built as a strong plan for the likely case plus something that still works if the player is somewhere else, instead of hedging every wave into blandness.

03

Difficulty and pacing

A fixed difficulty curve assumes everyone plays the same way, and they don't. So we built our own dynamic difficulty adjustment and I owned the tuning side of it.

Every parameter a designer can touch sits inside a category. Each category also has a few scoring parameters, and those give a score for the level you just played. The system looks at your last five levels, finds the categories you're scoring badly in, and eases those parameters. Categories you're scoring well in tighten up.

How a category works

PartWhat it isWhat it does
Tunable parametersThe values a designer sets for that part of the gameThe thing that actually changes
Scoring parametersA few measures of how the player handled that partProduces a score per level
AdjustmentRolling read of the last five levelsEases low scoring categories, tightens high ones
Play a level→ Score each category→ Compare last five→ Ease or tighten→ Next level

Each category moves on its own, so two players get different levels

Keeping the categories independent is what makes it useful. A player who dodges well but can't handle crowds gets thinner waves without losing the enemies that make them move. A player with the opposite problem gets the reverse. One global difficulty slider would have flattened both of them into the same middle.

It also changes what my job is. I'm not authoring one difficulty curve any more, I'm setting the range each category is allowed to move inside and making sure both ends of that range still play well.

04

Iteration

None of the three systems above started where they ended up.

The two jump rule came out of testing, not planning

Early layouts used the full six grids of height freely, because the kit allowed it. Rooms built that way read as separate ledges rather than one space, and fights turned into climbing. Capping normal traversal at two jumps fixed that, and it only became a rule worth breaking once it was reliable enough that players had actually learned it.

Wave placement started as guesswork

The first waves were placed on instinct, and they landed badly often enough that it was clearly a system problem rather than bad luck. Visual weight was the first fix and it helped, but it only worked on enemies that looked threatening. Adding the second lever, enemies that become priorities once you're fighting them, is what took it from a coin flip to something worth designing around.

The DDA changed what I tune

Before it existed I was tuning individual levels. After it, a level isn't a fixed thing any more, so tuning a single instance of it tells you very little. I had to start checking the eased and tightened ends of every category and asking whether both still made a good fight, which is slower but catches problems a single playthrough never would.

05

Final gameplay

The shipped game runs across four environment sets: dungeon, jungle, village and a moving bus. They reuse the same construction kit, so what changes between them is the shape of the room and the enemy mix rather than the rules.

A level plays out as a stack of the three systems. The layout decides where a player can stand and how fast they can cross the room. The wave decides where they want to stand. The DDA decides how hard that fight is for this particular player, based on the last five levels they played.

The moving bus is the clearest example of the kit being pushed. It's still one room with a fixed camera, but the floor is narrow and there's nowhere to fall back to, so waves that work in an open village square become far more dangerous without a single parameter changing.

The whole set is live on Google Play.
