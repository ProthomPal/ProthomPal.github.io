Case study 01 · Google Play · Casual time-management

Hitting a retention target, build by build

How successive builds of My Cooking Story resolved every major level-design wall found along the way and carried Day-1 retention past its target — one level, one funnel and one loading theory at a time.

SECTION 01

The starting point

My Cooking Story is a tycoon-style cooking and time-management game: players build a restaurant across sequential themes, preparing multi-layer recipes and serving customers within a level's timer and target. A slot system lets players cook ahead and store food before customers arrive, which is the skill ceiling the whole game is built around — plan early, serve fast.

The team's north-star metric was Day-1 retention against an internal benchmark — the share of new players who come back the day after installing.

My job was to move that number without a rewrite. That meant treating the level list as a tunable system rather than a fixed sequence of content, and finding out where the game was losing people before deciding what to change.

Every build answered one question and raised the next. That loop is the whole story.

SECTION 02

The working loop

Rather than shipping a batch of changes and hoping, each build was scoped to answer one question. Ship, read the funnel, find the deepest wall, retune it, re-measure. Slower per build, but it meant every result could be attributed to something specific instead of being lost in a pile of simultaneous edits.

One question per build

Re-checking the same levels build over build is what let each wall be caught rather than assumed fixed.

SECTION 03

Resolving the level-design walls

Three separate difficulty spikes surfaced across the builds, each at a different point in the early-to-mid game. Tracking the same levels every build shows each one being found and resolved in turn — and, in one case, being reintroduced by a change made elsewhere.

What a wall looks like in the completion line

Schematic. Illustrates the shape of the problem and the fix, not measured values.

The first wall — early onboarding

The baseline build had a hard stall in the first few levels, before players had learned the loop well enough to know what they were doing wrong. This is the most expensive place in the game to lose someone: they haven't seen a restaurant come together yet, so there's nothing pulling them back. Retuning the timer, customer target and order complexity here cleared it, and it has held ever since.

The second wall — a regression, not a new problem

A later build introduced a stall at a level that had been fine before. Nothing had been changed at that level directly; a tuning change further upstream had shifted what players arrived with. That's the argument for re-measuring the whole early game every build rather than only checking what you touched.

The third wall — deeper in the ramp

The last spike sat further into the mid-game, on a pair of consecutive levels. Because it came after the two earlier walls were resolved, more players were reaching it — which is exactly why fixing an early wall can make a later one look worse before it gets better. Retuning that pair was the change that finally moved the retention number.

Difficulty spikes need to be tracked, not just fixed once.

SECTION 04

Funnel depth

Fixing each wall shows up directly in how far players get before quitting. Comparing the same funnel across builds is the clearest way to see whether a level change actually bought progression or just moved the drop somewhere else.

How the funnel changed shape

SECTION 05

Design framework: reward pacing

Level design and reward cadence are tuned together, following a three-act structure across the game's level range. Onboarding rewards every clear to build habit; the mid-game spaces rewards out to create breathing room between pushes; the late game shifts to bigger, rarer payouts that reward mastery rather than frequency.

Reward cadence by act — the reward-frequency pattern the level list follows.

This cadence is deliberately asymmetric: dense, predictable rewards while players are still learning the core loop, widening as mastery increases, and a switch to weight-over-frequency in the endgame where players are optimising rather than discovering.

One rule sits underneath all of it — upgrade unlocks and new-content introductions are kept off the same level as each other wherever possible, so no single level asks a player to absorb two new things at once. Most of the early walls traced back to levels that broke this rule.

SECTION 06

Design framework: currency sources & sinks

The soft-currency loop follows a standard tycoon-economy structure: a small number of high-volume sources feed a small number of high-value sinks, keeping the loop legible without over-simplifying player choice.

The soft-currency loop, by weight

Sources
  1. Level-clear coins backbone
  2. Milestone chests interval
  3. Daily login bonus return hook

  1. Station upgrades primary

Ranked by share of currency earned and spent across a typical playthrough. Order shown; proportions not published.

On the source side, level-clear rewards are the backbone of the loop — a predictable, always-available baseline. Milestone chests sit on top at set intervals, a daily login bonus anchors return visits, and an optional ad-watch bonus is there for players who want to accelerate without paying.

On the sink side, station upgrades are deliberately the primary spend, because they visibly and immediately improve throughput. The player sees the restaurant get faster, which is what keeps the upgrade loop satisfying. Container upgrades follow, then speed boosters for players in a hurry, and item unlocks that gate new recipes behind progression.

SECTION 07

Investigation: ruling out load time

Early on, a drop in the onboarding funnel looked like it might be a load-time problem rather than a design one — a reasonable theory, and one that would have sent the work in a completely different direction. The build carried loading-time telemetry for dropped sessions, so it could be tested directly instead of argued about.

Testing the theory

Hypothesis

Players are dropping at level load because the game is slow to start a level.

Test

Load time recorded for every dropped session, then checked against each level's drop-off rate.

Verdict — ruled out

The overwhelming majority of dropped sessions loaded fast, with no correlation between a level's load time and its drop-off.

The few slow sessions were isolated, not concentrated on any particular level or device pattern. That closed the line of investigation with confidence and sent the work back to level tuning, where the actual problem was.

Data can rule out a theory as usefully as it confirms one.

SECTION 08

Where it landed

Every fix above has to answer one question: does it move the metric the team is actually held to? It did.

Day-1 retention cleared its internal target with room to spare, on an install cohort large enough that this reflects a genuine shift rather than a favourable sampling window. Most of the game's live markets clear the target independently, and the one that doesn't sits just short of it — which matters more than the headline, because it's the evidence that the underlying game moved, not a single lucky market.

The retention gain also arrived later than the level-design gain. The number held flat through the build that resolved the second wall, and only jumped once the third was fixed and the funnel got meaningfully deeper. Not every improvement shows up immediately in the top-line metric — which is an argument for tracking progression directly rather than judging every change by retention alone.

Outcome

My Cooking Story moved from an unstable early build to a validated, retention-positive live game. Every major level-design wall found in the process was resolved, and Day-1 retention now sits comfortably above its target.

SECTION 09

What I took from it

Track spikes, don't assume they stay fixed.

Three separate walls surfaced across the builds, one of them reintroduced by a change made somewhere else entirely. Re-checking the same levels every build is what caught it.

Ruling a theory out is real progress.

The load-time investigation produced no fix and no metric movement, and it was still one of the most useful things I did — it stopped the team spending a build on the wrong problem.

Fixing an early wall can make a later one look worse.

More players surviving the early game means more players arriving at the next spike. Progression data has to be read as a whole funnel, not level by level in isolation.

Compounding small fixes beat one big swing.

No single change carried the target. Scoping each build to one question is slower, but it's the only way to know which change did what.

Case study 02 · Creature collection · Mobile & Android TV

BR

From a pile of good mechanics to a system that holds together

Brainrot is a creature-collection game built for low-end living-room hardware. I was the only designer on a six-person team: I specified the systems and set the parameters, and a developer built them. I started with a list of appealing verbs and gradually learned to design around player behaviour, economy, clarity, performance and production cost. The game is the artifact — the change in how I think is the actual case study.

SECTION 01

What Brainrot is

The player moves through the world, encounters creatures, catches or steals them, fights in short timing-based encounters, levels them, and decides whether each one is worth keeping, merging or selling.

The core fantasy is acquisitive rather than heroic: everything I find is potentially mine, and everything I own is potentially worth more than it currently is. Ownership, upgrade and accumulation — not narrative triumph.

What made it difficult

Collection games are deceptively expensive. They demand volume of content, a progression system that stays meaningful across hundreds of hours, an economy that doesn't inflate, and a rendering budget that survives dozens of animated creatures at once. As the only designer on a six-person team, every one of those had to be reconciled by me — and the constraints regularly contradicted each other.

The game targets low-end living-room and mobile hardware. That single fact shaped more decisions than any aesthetic goal: input had to be minimal, encounters short, on-screen creature counts bounded, and content produced cheaply and reused aggressively.

SECTION 02

The original concept — and where it was naïve

The first version of the design was a list of verbs that sounded fun: explore the world, discover brainrots, catch or steal them, fight, level them up, sell or keep, buy eggs, build a collection.

Each seemed compelling on its own — and that was precisely the problem. They were compelling to me, imagining the game, rather than to a player in minute forty of session three. Discovery is exciting once; the fiftieth discovery is only exciting if the game has given the player a reason to evaluate it. Selling is only interesting if there's something worth buying. Collection is only motivating if the collection is doing something.

The naïveté wasn't in the individual mechanics. It was assuming a large enough pile of good mechanics would arrange itself into a good game.

SECTION 03

Core loop evolution

The original loop was short and structurally hollow. The developed loop isn't longer for its own sake — it introduces decision points instead of just more steps.

Before — a sequence of events

Explore→ Find→ Catch→ Fight→ Upgrade

Nothing here asks the player a question. A loop without questions is a treadmill.

After — a sequence of decisions

Explore→ Find brainrot→ Catch or steal→ QTE fight→ XP→ Level up who→ Sell, keep or merge→ Spend or hoard→ Buy egg

Loops back to explore — amber nodes are the decisions the player has to make

Is this one worth catching? Worth the HP cost? Worth keeping, or is it merge fodder? Do I spend now or save for an egg? Those questions are the game.

SECTION 04

Three foundational problems

A — "Finding a brainrot" was not enough

Discovery is a one-time thrill. Once a player has seen twenty creatures, a twenty-first means nothing unless the game gives them a way to evaluate it. The creature needed to be a proposition, not just an image.

So every creature got evaluable properties: HP, ATK, DEF, a QTE profile, rarity, a level, a tier multiplier, merge potential and collection value. A discovery became a small judgement rather than a small animation.

What this represents

This is the point where I stopped being a content designer — someone who adds things — and started being a systems designer — someone who defines how things relate to each other.

B — Combat had to be extremely simple

Combat is built on directional quick-time events. A correct, timely directional input means your brainrot attacks. A wrong input or a timeout means the wild one attacks. That's the whole system.

The lesson wasn't "I designed a QTE." It was working out what a combat system has to satisfy on constrained hardware and a constrained input device — the window widths, the input tolerances and the failure behaviour were mine to decide, and our developer built to them:

  • Low cognitive load — the player should never have to plan
  • Immediate feedback — input and consequence in the same beat
  • Short encounters — combat is a punctuation mark, not a chapter
  • Unambiguous success and failure states
  • Minimal controls — a TV remote has no room for a combat grammar

Interaction cost is a design budget. Every input I ask for has to buy something the player values more than the effort of giving it.

C — Progression could become meaningless

I added levels, XP, rarity, tiers, merging and multipliers. Each addition made numbers go up — which surfaced the real question: if everything increases power, what actually matters?

Numerical progression

A bigger number. A level-up that only raises ATK is arithmetic.

Meaningful progression

A number that changes what the player can decide to do. A level-up that crosses the merge threshold is a door opening.

Tiers, merge thresholds and rarity ceilings exist to convert arithmetic into decisions.

SECTION 05

The tier system: turning excess into progression

Identical brainrots merge into the next tier, and merging requires a minimum level. Both rules took iteration to arrive at.

The tier ladder

Base
Silver
Gold
Diamond
Shiny
God

Identical creatures merge upward. A God-tier creature is visible from the first hour and unreachable for a long time — which is exactly what a long-term goal should be.

Why identical creatures merge

Collection games generate duplicates relentlessly, and the default player experience of a duplicate is mild disappointment. Merging inverts that: a duplicate is no longer a failed roll, it's half of an upgrade.

Why merging needs a level threshold

Without one, merging is pure inventory management and can be done instantly on acquisition. The level requirement forces investment before consolidation, which does three things at once: it slows tier inflation, it makes each merge feel earned, and it routes duplicates back through the XP loop instead of around it.

Lesson

Excess content is not a problem to be prevented. It's a currency waiting for a system that can spend it.

SECTION 06

Healing: making damage matter without punishing attachment

The naive version restores HP after every fight. Convenient — and it makes combat consequence-free. Losing costs the player nothing but a few seconds.

The system that replaced it has weight: damage persists after combat, HP regenerates over real time, healing centres offer accelerated recovery, and a revive threshold governs what happens when a creature falls.

Finding the position between two bad extremes

The loss is real, but the cost is measured in patience rather than in the collection itself. Healing centres are the pressure valve.

The result is a genuine four-way tension between risk, recovery, resource management and attachment — and each player resolves it differently depending on how much they care about the particular creature that got hurt.

SECTION 07

Stealing and shields: social tension without multiplayer

A collection that only ever grows has no stakes. Players needed a reason to care about the world beyond accumulation — something that could take from them. So: stealing. If brainrots can be stolen, owning one is a position to be defended rather than a permanent fact.

The moment theft became possible, protection became necessary — not as a nicety but as counterplay. A risk system without counterplay isn't tension, it's arbitrary loss.

The ownership cycle

Acquire→ Protect→ Risk→ Lose→ Recover

Ownership becomes a position to defend, not a fact to accumulate

The constraint that makes this interesting is that the game has no real-time online multiplayer. The design had to manufacture social pressure — the feeling of a contested world — without the infrastructure, latency budget or player population that genuine multiplayer requires. Producing the emotional output of multiplayer without its technical input was one of the more demanding constraint-driven decisions in the project.

SECTION 08

Milestones: presence is not comprehension

The milestone system gives players objectives and claimable rewards. It worked, technically. Players still forgot it existed.

The problem wasn't the objectives — it was their presentation. Unclaimed rewards weren't visible enough at the moments a player would care, objective sequencing was jagged rather than smoothly escalating, and the whole system sat outside the player's habitual attention path.

A reward the player never claims is not a reward. It's a feature that consumed development time and returned nothing.

This was the point at which UX stopped being a layer applied on top of my design and became part of the design itself.

SECTION 09

Economy and currency sinks

The economy accumulated pieces over time — selling brainrots, buying eggs, purchasing shields, healing costs, continue currency, upgrade costs. The important shift wasn't adding those pieces. It was realising what they had to add up to.

Currency generation is not an economy

Sources→ Storage→ Decisions→ Sinks→ Scarcity→ Motivation

Break any link and the circuit stops working.

Sources without sinks

Inflation, then indifference. A player with more currency than uses has effectively finished the game without being told.

Sinks without decisions

A tax rather than an economy. The player pays, but nothing about the payment is interesting.

What I was really designing wasn't a shop. It was the shape of scarcity — and therefore the shape of what the player wants.

SECTION 10

Retention as a design constraint

The most significant change in how I worked came from specifying what the game needed to measure, then reading what players actually did with it. I defined the events and the funnel steps; our developer wired them up. I stopped asking only "is this fun?" and started asking "where are players leaving?"

Measuring Day-1 retention reframed the work entirely. The number stopped being a verdict on quality and became a diagnostic prompt: what does the design need to accomplish in the first session for a player to return the next day, and is it accomplishing it?

Before

"I think players will like this." Content added because it seemed appealing. Analytics as a post-launch report.

After

"The data says players behave this way — what design decision could explain it?" Content added against an identified behavioural gap. Analytics as evidence inside the design process.

SECTION 11

Designing around drop-off

The QTE tutorial drop-off

The tutorial was teaching too much at once. Every individual thing it taught was correct; collectively it exceeded what a new player could absorb in their first minutes. The fix wasn't better wording — it was fewer simultaneous concepts.

Teaching mechanics is not the same as teaching understanding. A player who has been shown something has not necessarily learned it.

The connectivity popup

An apparently external interruption — a connectivity prompt — produced measurable friction at a specific point in the funnel. It wasn't gameplay, and by my original mental model it wasn't my problem.

Anything that interrupts the loop is part of the player experience, regardless of whether the designer classifies it as "gameplay." The player doesn't distinguish between a bad level and a badly-timed dialog. They only experience the interruption.

Progression drop-off

I moved from looking at aggregate retention to looking at individual levels — the transition from a headline number to funnel analysis. Aggregate retention tells you that something is wrong. Per-level funnels tell you where, and "where" is the only form of the information you can act on.

SECTION 12

When performance became game design

Brainrot wasn't designed on an unlimited PC. On the target hardware, frame rate degraded severely when many brainrots were on screen — which is to say, precisely when the game was doing the thing it exists to do.

Our developer profiled it and worked through animation cost, double-sided materials, debug drawing, shadow rendering, overall rendering cost and vertex limits. My part was the design side of the same problem: deciding how many creatures could be on screen at once, when they were visible, and what the collection could look like within whatever budget the profiling left us. The significant outcome wasn't the optimisation work.

On-screen creature count is a design parameter, not a technical detail

Schematic. Illustrates the trade-off, not measured performance data.

If fifty creatures look fantastic and destroy the frame rate, the design is not fantastic. It's an expensive slideshow.

The collection fantasy had to be delivered within a rendering budget, which meant designing presentation — how a large collection is displayed, when creatures are visible, how tiers are communicated — around what the hardware could actually sustain. A game designer working on constrained hardware cannot separate design from technology.

SECTION 13

Production-conscious design

Later design work accounted for factors the earlier work simply ignored: low-end hardware, total asset count, per-creature animation cost, cheap content generation, on-screen limits, reusable systems, tier variations as content multipliers, and overall content scalability.

Tier variations are the clearest example. Six tiers over a shared creature base multiplies perceived content without multiplying asset production — a decision made in direct response to a production constraint that nonetheless improved the game rather than merely economising on it.

The question I used to ask

Can I design this?

The question I ask now

Can I actually produce and run this?

On a six-person team that distinction isn't a refinement. A design that can't be produced within the team's capacity or run on the target device isn't an ambitious design — it's a schedule problem I've handed to everyone else.

SECTION 14

Evolution as a designer

01
Feature-oriented

"What would be fun to put into the game?" Strong at idea generation — but mechanics could exist without a clear purpose, and nothing in my process would catch that.

02
Systems designer

"How do these mechanics interact?" No longer isolated features but interdependent systems, where changing one forces changes in others.

03
Economy designer

"What enters, where does it go, why does the player spend, and what happens when they have too much?" Player motivation as a function of scarcity rather than content volume.

04
UX / behavioural designer

"Do players understand it, use it, and come back because of it?" Watching what players actually did rather than what I intended them to do.

05
Data-driven designer

"Where does the funnel break?" Analytics moved from something appended after development to evidence used during it.

06
Constraint-driven designer

"What's the best experience I can create within the actual constraints of this game?" Rather than what the perfect game would look like if hardware, budget and time didn't exist.

SECTION 15

What Brainrot taught me

Lesson 01

A mechanic isn't valuable because it's fun in isolation. It's valuable because it strengthens the overall loop.

Lesson 02

Player behaviour is more reliable evidence than designer assumption.

Lesson 03

Progression needs emotional meaning, not just larger numbers.

Lesson 04

Every system creates another system problem. Stealing creates a need for protection; protection creates scarcity; scarcity creates economy requirements; economy creates sinks. The skill is anticipating the next link rather than being surprised by it.

Lesson 05

Clarity is a mechanic. If players don't understand a system, that system effectively doesn't exist.

Lesson 06

Performance is part of design. A beautiful mechanic that can't run on the target device isn't a successful mechanic.

Lesson 07

The achievement isn't getting the first design right — it's getting better at identifying why the first design was wrong.

01

Level design

A side-on brawler gives you one axis of movement and a camera that gates progress, so the design work is about what the player can see coming and how much room they have when it arrives. Each environment theme changes that arithmetic: a dungeon corridor pins you against walls, a jungle gives you verticality, a village opens the space out, and a moving bus removes retreat entirely.

Add here: your layout approach in your own words — how you spaced pickups and obstacles, where you placed the camera locks, and how you made a space readable the moment the player enters it.
02

Encounter design

Enemies range from low-level street goons to bosses, and the interesting work is composition rather than count — which enemy types are on screen together, in what order they arrive, and which player skill each wave is asking for. Boosters like shields, double punch and magic jump are part of that equation: where they're placed decides whether a wave reads as a threat or a power fantasy.

Add here: one encounter broken down beat by beat — spawn order, timing, what the player is meant to learn from it.
03

Difficulty & pacing

Intensity is shaped across the level set rather than within a single level: peaks at boss encounters and theme transitions, rests immediately after, and a slow widening of the enemy mix so new types get introduced against familiar ones.
